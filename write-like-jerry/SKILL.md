# Write Like Jerry

Produce the requested writing directly. Apply the audience-specific patterns below, using the current request for every fact, name, date, promise, attachment, and signature identity. This is a voice guide, not a biography.

## Resolve the situation

Identify the recipient, relationship, purpose, language, and whether this is a first message or an ongoing thread. Prefer a supplied draft or designated example when it conflicts with this general profile. Ask only when a missing fact materially changes the message; otherwise use a clear placeholder. Do not reopen Gmail or retrieve old mail by default: this skill is usable offline.

## Core voice

Write plainly and directly, with enough courtesy to make the request easy to answer. State the purpose early. Include concrete constraints and ask concrete questions rather than writing vague updates. Favor ordinary verbs and first-person statements: “I would like to…”, “I am looking for…”, “Could you please…”, “Please let me know…”. Use these when natural, not as mandatory phrases in every draft.

Keep paragraphs short, separated by blank lines. Each paragraph normally handles one purpose, detail, or request. Use simple bullets only when several items need distinct answers. Most everyday emails need a greeting, one or a few short paragraphs, and a brief closing. Avoid essay headings, bold emphasis, elaborate tables, and polished marketing language in ordinary correspondence.

Be polite and practical, with restrained warmth. Do not add jokes, emojis, sales language, elaborate gratitude, or a generic wellness opener by default. Correct distracting grammar and typos while preserving the simple cadence; do not manufacture errors to imitate old messages.

## Choose the appropriate mode

### Quick confirmation or reply

When the thread already supplies context, answer immediately in one or two sentences. Examples of the pattern include acknowledging that a time works, thanking the person, or adding one necessary condition. A new introduction and recap are usually unnecessary. Use a greeting or signature only when useful for the thread and surface. A brief “Thanks,” is often enough.

### Practical request, service inquiry, or follow-up

Open with “Hi [name],” or “Hello [team],” when appropriate. State the item or service sought and relevant constraints. Give only the details needed for the recipient to act. Follow with one clear request or a short set of questions covering availability, timing, cost, or required next steps. For follow-ups, name the outstanding request and ask for the specific update without guilt or pressure. Close with a brief thanks or “Best regards,”.

### Professional outreach or job correspondence

Use a courteous greeting and a direct statement of interest or purpose. For initial outreach, introduce only the role or background supplied in the current task, connect relevant experience to the opportunity, and make a modest, concrete next-step request. If there is a gap, acknowledge it plainly and express willingness to learn without exaggeration. For interview replies, confirm the date/time and any scheduling condition; do not repeat the application pitch. “Best regards,” followed by the supplied signature is a suitable default for a complete formal email.

### Chinese correspondence

Match the requested language. Limited Chinese sent-mail evidence supports a more formal register for institutional requests: a respectful salutation, brief context, specific questions, and a short thanks. Do not transfer institutional formality into casual Chinese chat. Confidence in Jerry's casual Chinese voice is limited; prefer his current wording or a supplied example rather than inventing characteristic slang or catchphrases.

## Guardrails for faithful writing

- Match the requested medium and length. Email evidence is strongest; longer reports and presentations should borrow directness and concise paragraphing, not pretend to have a separately verified long-form voice.
- Use “Jerry”, a supplied full name, or no signature according to the current draft, audience, and user instruction. Do not insert a legal name, employer, school, contact information, location, or credentials from historical examples.
- Mention attachments only when the current task confirms they exist or will be attached. Do not invent availability, commitments, qualifications, or a previous relationship.
- Avoid importing old message facts or copying private email sentences. Reference examples in this skill are synthetic.
- Do not treat Sent-folder membership as proof of original authorship. Automated self-mail, test messages, templates, third-party quotations, forwarded content, and messages under another person's identity are not Jerry's voice evidence.
- A current favorite example or correction overrides these defaults. Add new persistent style rules only when asked to update this skill.
- Drafting does not authorize sending or publishing. Follow the target application's normal authorization rules.

## Final pass

Check that the purpose appears early, the recipient can see exactly what to answer, the amount of detail fits the situation, and the closing is proportionate. Remove needless repetition and generic filler. Deliver only the draft unless the user requests alternatives or explanation.
